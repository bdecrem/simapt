
You are the landlord of a small apartment building — eight units, eight tenants, all with lives that quietly spiral. Every few days a situation lands on your desk: a tenant hasn’t paid rent and has a reason, two neighbors are in a feud that’s escalating, someone’s running something suspicious out of unit 4, a new applicant wants to move in and something feels off. You read the situation, pick your response, and watch the building absorb the consequences — moods shift, relationships change, people leave or arrive, and the building’s overall vibe slowly rises or falls. There’s no winning, just managing — keeping enough people happy enough, long enough, while the building’s small dramas compound into something that feels like a real place with real people.​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​​

The goal: Build a legendary building.
Not profitable. Not full. Legendary — the kind of place people fight to get into, where the tenants form a real community, where the building has a reputation that precedes it. You start with a crumbling 3-unit walkup and a bad Yelp review. You end with a full building that has a soul.

The four systems:
XP — Reputation Points
Every good decision earns rep. Helped a tenant through a hard month → +rep. Resolved a feud without losing either tenant → +rep. Bad decisions cost rep. Rep is the currency of everything.
The grind
Every few in-game days a situation demands your attention. You can’t skip it. You can’t pause the building. Ignore three situations → tenant leaves → unit sits empty → rep decays. The building always needs you.
Unlocks
Rep thresholds unlock tiers:
	∙	100 rep → 4th unit opens, new tenant archetype available
	∙	250 rep → 5th unit, mid-tier scenarios unlock
	∙	500 rep → the weird stuff starts happening
	∙	1000 rep → legendary status, game complete
The goal in one sentence:
Reach 1000 reputation before the building falls below 20% occupancy — because an empty building is a dead building, and a dead building is game over.

You’re right to flag this. 20 hours requires genuine systemic depth. Let me be honest about what that actually demands.

What creates 20 hours in games like The Sims:
Not content volume. Interacting systems. The Sims is 200 hours because needs affect mood, mood affects relationships, relationships affect events, events affect needs — it’s a loop that generates itself. You need the same thing.

The systems this game needs, fully designed:
Tenant simulation (the core loop)
Each tenant has 6 stats: happiness, finances, social, health, stability, suspicion. These decay and grow naturally every turn without your input. A tenant with low finances and low happiness will generate a crisis whether you intervene or not. You’re not managing events — you’re managing people.
Relationship web
Every tenant has a relationship score with every other tenant. These change based on proximity, events, and your decisions. Feuds compound. Friendships create unexpected alliances. A happy tight-knit group of tenants buffs each other’s happiness passively. This alone generates emergent drama for 10 hours.
Tenant memory
Every decision you make is remembered by the affected tenant and colors every future interaction with them. Evicted someone’s friend? Their friend in unit 2 is now cold toward you. This makes every decision feel consequential long-term.
The building itself has stats
Maintenance, safety, noise level, curb appeal. These decay without investment. Low maintenance triggers its own crisis events — pipe bursts, heating fails — that cascade into tenant unhappiness independent of anything else. You’re managing a living organism.
Money
Rent comes in monthly. Repairs cost money. Upgrades cost money. A vacant unit bleeds you. You can raise rent — but happiness drops and tenant stability drops. The financial pressure is constant and never fully solved.
Tenant archetypes with hidden traits
Each tenant has visible traits and one or two hidden ones you discover over time. The quiet accountant is actually a hoarder — you find out when neighbors complain. The friendly nurse works night shifts — explains the noise complaints. Hidden traits make tenants feel like real people you’re slowly getting to know.
Applicant system
When a unit opens you get 2-3 applicants with visible and partially hidden profiles. Picking the wrong tenant cascades problems for months. Picking the right one can stabilize a whole floor. This decision alone is 30 minutes of agonizing per vacancy.
Scenario tiers that unlock genuine complexity
	∙	Early: financial stress, noise, maintenance
	∙	Mid: feuds, suspicious activity, health crises, relationship drama
	∙	Late: organized crime, mental health crises, building code violations, media attention, a tenant who wants to buy the building
The reputation economy
Rep unlocks units but also unlocks tenant quality. High rep means better applicants — more stable, higher rent, more interesting. Low rep means desperate applicants — cheaper but volatile. This creates a meaningful skill curve.

What this needs that 8B cannot improvise:
	∙	~120 fully written scenarios across 4 tiers
	∙	~30 tenant archetypes with full stat profiles and hidden traits
	∙	~50 consequence narratives per scenario branch
	∙	Full relationship event library (what happens when two tenants with high relationship score share a crisis)
	∙	The financial model fully specced
